01 — Interests

How I Spend My Time

🕮
Reading

My reading spans urban history, philosophy of science, Persian classical literature, and contemporary fiction. I am drawn to books that take ideas seriously — Ibn Khaldun on cities and civilizations, Jane Jacobs on urban vitality, Italo Calvino on invisible cities. Reading is not separate from research; it is how I think.

Urban TheoryPhilosophyPersian LiteratureHistory
🏃
Running

Early morning runs through Toyohashi — along the Toyokawa river, through the market streets before they wake up. Running is how I process problems: the first 2km is still thinking about yesterday's data, the next 3km is where ideas arrive. Some of my best modelling decisions happened mid-run.

Travel

Every city I have visited for a conference becomes a fieldwork site. I walk the public transport networks, photograph the spatial distribution of commercial activity, and observe how people move. Antwerp, Montreal, Surakarta, Singapore, Bangkok, Baguio — each one a living data point for urban theory.

Coding

Python for data analysis and microsimulation, R for spatial statistics and econometrics, some JavaScript for visualisation and web tools. Coding is not separate from research — it is where abstract models meet computational reality. I enjoy building things that work, even ugly ones.

PythonRGISSimulation

Photography

Urban photography — the way light falls on infrastructure, the texture of sidewalks, the geometry of transit stations at 6am. I am interested in cities as visual systems, not just data systems. Photography makes me look at urban space more carefully than any research instrument.

03 — Reading

Books That Shaped My Thinking

The Death and Life of Great American Cities
Jane Jacobs, 1961

The book that made me understand cities as living systems, not engineering problems. Jacobs' critique of top-down urban renewal feels more relevant than ever in the age of smart city techno-optimism.

Urban Theory
The Muqaddimah
Ibn Khaldun, 1377

The 14th-century Tunisian philosopher's theory of urban cycles, social cohesion (asabiyyah), and the rise and fall of civilizations. The first systematic attempt at social science — and still remarkably insightful on how cities form and decay.

History & Philosophy
Invisible Cities
Italo Calvino, 1972

Marco Polo describes 55 imaginary cities to Kublai Khan — each one a different way of thinking about what a city is. I return to this every time I need to remind myself that cities are fundamentally about human imagination, not just transport networks.

Fiction
Thinking, Fast and Slow
Daniel Kahneman, 2011

Essential reading for anyone building models of human behavior. Kahneman's work on bounded rationality and cognitive biases directly informs how I think about agent decision rules in microsimulation.

Behavioural Science
The Remains of the Day
Kazuo Ishiguro, 1989

A novel about dedication, regret, and the stories we tell ourselves about our lives. I read it during my first year in Japan — it made me think about what it means to commit fully to something, whether a profession or a set of values.

Fiction
Triumph of the City
Edward Glaeser, 2011

The economic case for density and urbanization, argued rigorously and with genuine affection for cities. Not uncritical reading — Glaeser's techno-optimism has limits — but essential for understanding why cities matter economically.

04 — Values

What I Believe In

🏭
Cities as Common Good

The city belongs to everyone who lives in it. Research that improves urban systems must be grounded in this — not optimizing for GDP alone, but for the quality of daily life of the people who use the city, especially those who have the fewest alternatives.

📈
Rigour Without Ivory Tower

Academic rigour matters — but the purpose of research is to change things, not to accumulate citations. I try to work on problems that have clear policy relevance and to present findings in ways that are useful to practitioners, not just peer reviewers.

🌎
Cross-Cultural Thinking

Growing up between Kabul and Toyohashi, working across Japan, Thailand, and the Philippines, has taught me that good urban theory must be cross-cultural. Cities in Asia age differently from European cities. Digital adoption patterns differ by culture. Our models must reflect this.
